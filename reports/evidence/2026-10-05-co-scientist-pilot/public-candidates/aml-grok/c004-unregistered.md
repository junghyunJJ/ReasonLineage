Title: Teriflunomide at multiple-sclerosis exposures induces uridine-rescuable myeloid differentiation in differentiation-blocked acute myeloid leukemia

Hypothesis. Teriflunomide, a dihydroorotate dehydrogenase (DHODH) inhibitor whose approval for relapsing forms of multiple sclerosis is unverified prior knowledge and must be confirmed on the product label, will at free concentrations documented for that indication relieve the myeloid differentiation blockade in primary acute myeloid leukemia (AML) blasts that depend on de novo pyrimidine synthesis. The primary endpoint is differentiation, read out by morphology and mature myeloid surface markers, with a secondary reduction in leukemic clonogenicity. Bulk cytotoxicity, eradication of reactive-oxygen-species-low leukemia stem cells, and sensitization of cytarabine-residual oxidative cells are separate biological contexts and are outside the claimed mechanism.

Supported premises. DHODH inhibition promotes myeloid differentiation in experimental AML systems and affects disease-related endpoints in the models tested [S4]. That record links a defined metabolic enzyme to a differentiation outcome and supplies an alternative to nonspecific cell killing [S4]. The same record states that the result does not establish that every approved drug affecting this pathway reproduces the phenotype or achieves suitable exposure and selectivity [S4]. Functionally defined leukemia stem cells in the specimens studied often had low reactive oxygen species and elevated BCL-2, and BCL-2 inhibition reduced oxidative phosphorylation and affected quiescent leukemia stem cells [S1]. Residual cells after cytarabine in the models examined were not consistently enriched for immature quiescent leukemia stem cells; they displayed oxidative-metabolism and fatty-acid-oxidation-associated features [S2]. Venetoclax with azacitidine is an established regimen connected to leukemia-stem-cell TCA-cycle and oxidative-phosphorylation changes [S3]. Those three records set boundaries: this hypothesis concerns DHODH-linked differentiation [S4], held apart from BCL-2-linked stem-cell oxidative phosphorylation [S1], from cytarabine-residual oxidative metabolism [S2], and from the venetoclax–azacitidine comparator [S3].

Speculative bridges. The step from [S4] to teriflunomide is an assumption. It assumes that teriflunomide inhibits DHODH in human AML blasts, that unbound marrow concentrations at approved multiple-sclerosis doses overlap the range that engaged DHODH in the experimental systems of [S4], and that pyrimidine depletion in those blasts is sufficient to engage maturation rather than only slowing proliferation. It also assumes a usable window versus normal CD34-positive progenitors and versus nonhematopoietic tissues that require de novo pyrimidine synthesis. Exposure, protein binding, marrow penetration, selectivity, and drug identity are absent from [S1]–[S4]. They remain unverified prior knowledge until checked against the label and primary pharmacokinetic sources. Leflunomide, a prodrug used in rheumatoid arthritis, is related by unverified prior knowledge as a source of teriflunomide; this proposal tests teriflunomide itself so the active species is explicit.

Alternative explanations. An apparent maturation phenotype could be nonspecific stress or loss of immature markers during cell death. Off-target activities reported for leflunomide-related chemistry are unverified prior knowledge and could contribute if they operate at the concentrations used. Maturation might require a particular baseline differentiation state and appear only in a subset of AML, so a single responsive model would leave the context claim unresolved. A lower leukemic burden could also arise from antiproliferative nucleotide depletion without the lineage maturation described in [S4]. Salvage of extracellular pyrimidines from the microenvironment could bypass DHODH and remove the phenotype in vivo while it remains visible in nucleotide-poor medium.

Discriminating preclinical test. Pair primary AML samples that retain a differentiation block in short-term culture with normal CD34-positive hematopoietic cells cultured in the same wave. Include a tool DHODH inhibitor that reproduces the differentiation result of [S4] as a mechanistic positive control, plus vehicle. Use venetoclax–azacitidine only as a separate comparator on a stem-enriched or ROS-low fraction, scored for viability and oxidative-phosphorylation-linked readouts [S1][S3], so that endpoint stays distinct from differentiation. Anchor teriflunomide concentrations to published total and unbound plasma levels for the multiple-sclerosis indication; those numbers are unverified here and must come from primary sources. Prespecify the primary endpoint as morphologic maturation together with CD11b and CD14, or equivalent mature myeloid markers, and record cell-cycle distribution and viability as secondary measures. Exogenous uridine is the on-target rescue: restoration of downstream pyrimidines should reverse DHODH target engagement and a DHODH-dependent maturation effect. A washout arm asks whether maturation persists after drug removal, which would favor differentiation over reversible cytostasis. Add clonogenic assays and, where cell numbers allow, a limited patient-derived xenograft or stromal co-culture for leukemic burden and normal-lineage output. Measure dihydroorotate accumulation or a pyrimidine-nucleotide ratio as the target-engagement biomarker. A change in oxygen consumption is reserved for the oxidative contexts in [S1]–[S3] and is not evidence for this claim.

Falsifying observation. The drug–mechanism–context claim is disconfirmed if, at concentrations corresponding to documented unbound multiple-sclerosis exposures, teriflunomide yields no increase in myeloid maturation relative to vehicle in the same primary samples in which the tool DHODH inhibitor does produce maturation. It is also disconfirmed if maturation occurs while exogenous uridine reverses neither the DHODH-engagement biomarker nor the maturation phenotype. The selectivity claim is disconfirmed if normal CD34-positive cells lose clonogenicity or viability to an equal or greater degree at those concentrations. A cytotoxic pattern without maturation, or a phenotype confined to concentrations far above documented human exposures, falsifies the translational form of the hypothesis even if a high-concentration laboratory effect remains.

Limitations. [S4] used experimental systems and does not certify clinical exposure, marrow penetration, or a therapeutic index for any approved DHODH drug. High protein binding, if confirmed in primary pharmacokinetic literature, could leave free levels below the differentiation threshold. Activated lymphocytes and gastrointestinal epithelium depend on de novo pyrimidine synthesis, so tolerability in multiple sclerosis may predict poorly in cytopenic AML. Cytarabine-residual oxidative metabolism [S2] and ROS-low stem-cell BCL-2 dependence [S1] could persist after a differentiating stimulus and would require separate studies. Genotype, prior hypomethylating-agent exposure, and pyrimidine salvage can restrict the responding subset. This proposal does not assert clinical efficacy.
