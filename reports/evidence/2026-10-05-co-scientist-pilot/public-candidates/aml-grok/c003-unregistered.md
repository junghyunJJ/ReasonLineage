Title: Teriflunomide for differentiation-blocked non-APL AML via on-target dihydroorotate dehydrogenase inhibition.

Hypothesis. Teriflunomide, whose original approved indication is relapsing multiple sclerosis (unverified prior knowledge; leflunomide, of which teriflunomide is the active metabolite, is approved for rheumatoid arthritis and likewise requires verification), will inhibit dihydroorotate dehydrogenase in differentiation-blocked non-APL acute myeloid leukemia blasts and induce myeloid maturation at free-drug exposures already achieved in that neurologic indication. The primary biological endpoint is differentiation, recorded separately from bulk-cell killing and from eradication of quiescent leukemia stem cells.

Supported premises. DHODH inhibition promoted myeloid differentiation in experimental AML systems and affected disease-related endpoints in the models investigated, connecting pyrimidine metabolism with relief of differentiation blockade and supplying an alternative to nonspecific cell killing [S4]. The same source does not establish that every approved drug affecting this pathway reproduces that result or achieves suitable exposure and selectivity [S4]. In the specimens studied, functionally defined leukemia stem cells frequently had low reactive oxygen species and elevated BCL-2, and BCL-2 inhibition reduced oxidative phosphorylation and affected quiescent leukemia stem cells [S1]. Venetoclax plus azacitidine is an established clinical and mechanistic comparator that has been linked to changes in leukemia-stem-cell TCA-cycle and oxidative-phosphorylation metabolism; repeating that regimen is outside this proposal [S3]. Cytarabine-residual human AML cells in the models examined displayed mitochondrial and fatty-acid-oxidation-related oxidative metabolism and were a population distinct from a consistent enrichment of immature quiescent leukemia stem cells [S2]. Those boundaries motivate a differentiation endpoint in a stated non-APL blast context rather than a claim of universal stem-cell eradication.

Speculative bridges. It is unverified prior knowledge, outside [S1]–[S4], that teriflunomide inhibits DHODH, is approved for multiple sclerosis, and attains defined plasma levels. Unverified prior knowledge also holds that DHODH oxidizes dihydroorotate to orotate and can pass electrons toward the respiratory chain, so blockade might couple pyrimidine depletion to a respiratory change; whether any teriflunomide effect follows the differentiation path in [S4], the OXPHOS path in [S1], or both is a prediction, not a reported result. Bridging [S4] to this molecule assumes that clinically used exposure occupies blast DHODH enough to limit de novo pyrimidine synthesis as tool inhibitors did in those experiments. A further assumption is that differentiation blockade in primary non-APL AML, across more than the genotypes already studied, remains DHODH-dependent. Selectivity assumes that normal hematopoietic progenitors compensate through pyrimidine salvage at the same exposure; [S1]–[S4] do not measure teriflunomide in normal marrow. Extension of the drug to cytarabine-residual cells [S2] or to ROS-low stem cells [S1] is a new prediction.

Alternative explanations. A rise in mature myeloid markers can reflect death of immature blasts with persistence of pre-existing mature cells. Anti-leukemic activity can come from off-target suppression of oxidative phosphorylation, aligning with the metabolic effects described in [S1] and [S3], or from interference with fatty-acid oxidation in residual cells [S2]. Nucleotide stress can slow proliferation in the absence of maturation. Immunomodulatory effects or organ toxicities associated with the approved uses could change in vivo disease burden through mechanisms other than blast DHODH.

Discriminating preclinical strategy. Test primary differentiation-blocked non-APL AML blasts side by side with healthy donor CD34-positive cells. Include a venetoclax–azacitidine comparator arm only to map oxidative-phosphorylation and TCA changes [S3]. At viability-matched time points, quantify morphology and myeloid surface-marker maturation, clonogenic frequency, and leukemogenic output in xenograft or co-culture, and pair these with respirometry so differentiation is scored separately from OXPHOS suppression [S1]. In parallel arms, compare ROS-low and ROS-higher fractions [S1] and cytarabine-residual versus treatment-naive cells [S2]. The on-target discriminator is exogenous uridine, treated here as unverified prior knowledge of a salvage bypass of DHODH: on-target differentiation should be prevented by uridine, whereas an off-target mitochondrial effect should persist. Anchor concentrations to verified human free teriflunomide levels, and label any higher in vitro concentration as supratherapeutic.

Falsifying observation. The proposed drug–mechanism–context relationship is falsified if verified clinically relevant concentrations fail to produce uridine-preventable myeloid differentiation in the pre-specified differentiation-blocked non-APL samples, or if normal CD34-positive cells show a matching loss of viability and of maturation capacity. It is likewise falsified if leukemia control occurs only as uridine-insensitive oxidative-phosphorylation collapse matching the venetoclax–azacitidine pattern [S3], without a maturation signature. Activity confined to a single genotype falsifies the broad non-APL claim.

Limitations. A pathway link in [S4] is insufficient evidence of efficacy, exposure, or a therapeutic window for teriflunomide. Results on BCL-2 dependence, venetoclax–azacitidine metabolism, and cytarabine-residual oxidative metabolism stay inside their own contexts [S1]–[S3]. Approval status, protein binding, free fraction, and drug–drug interaction risk remain unverified prior knowledge. Pyrimidine salvage, DHODH resistance, and toxicities known from the original indication can break translation. This proposal asserts a testable preclinical relationship and does not claim clinical benefit.
