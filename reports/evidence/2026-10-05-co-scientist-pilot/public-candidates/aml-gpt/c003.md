# c003

Teriflunomide-induced differentiation of pyrimidine-salvage-poor residual AML after cytarabine

Hypothesis: At clinically attainable unbound exposure, teriflunomide will inhibit DHODH and induce durable myeloid differentiation in cytarabine-residual AML cells that retain oxidative metabolism but have insufficient pyrimidine-salvage capacity. This will reduce leukemia-propagating activity while preserving normal hematopoietic progenitor function. The proposed context is functionally defined residual disease, not all AML or an assumed leukemia-stem-cell population.

Supported premises: Cytarabine-resistant AML cells displayed oxidative-metabolism-associated properties but were not consistently enriched for immature, quiescent leukemia stem cells [S2]. Separately, DHODH inhibition promoted differentiation in experimental AML systems [S4]. These findings justify testing a metabolic differentiation strategy in residual disease, but do not establish that oxidative residual cells depend on DHODH. ROS-low leukemia stem cells and their BCL-2-associated metabolic vulnerability represent a distinct population requiring separate functional assessment [S1]. Venetoclax–azacitidine provides an established energy-metabolism comparator, not the proposed repurposing discovery [S3].

Unverified prior knowledge: Teriflunomide is recalled as an approved treatment for relapsing forms of multiple sclerosis and as a DHODH inhibitor with substantial plasma-protein binding. These approval, original-indication, pharmacology, and binding facts require independent verification because the packet does not establish them. Human exposure, marrow penetration, and safety constraints likewise need checking before translational interpretation. No first-in-AML discovery claim is made.

Speculative bridges: The proposal assumes that a subset of oxidative residual AML relies sufficiently on de novo pyrimidine synthesis that DHODH inhibition cannot be compensated by salvage. It further assumes that teriflunomide can engage this dependency at tolerable exposure and that nucleotide restriction produces maturation rather than merely arrest or death. Oxidative metabolism would be an enrichment context, not a surrogate proof of DHODH dependence. Selectivity is a prediction to test, not an inference from the multiple-sclerosis indication or from [S4].

Discriminating preclinical strategy: Study paired pretreatment and post-cytarabine patient specimens, complemented by independently derived residual-disease models. Before treatment, classify samples by oxygen consumption and isotope-based de novo versus salvage pyrimidine flux under measured, physiologically relevant uridine and protein conditions. Include salvage-competent residual AML, untreated AML, and normal donor hematopoietic progenitors. Test concentration–time profiles bounded by independently verified human unbound exposure; measure intracellular drug exposure, DHODH inhibition, pyrimidine pools, and early metabolic changes before substantial cell loss.

Compare vehicle, teriflunomide, and inducible partial DHODH suppression, with uridine-rescue arms. A catalytically functional, teriflunomide-insensitive DHODH rescue construct, if independently validated, would strengthen target attribution; uridine rescue alone would establish pyrimidine dependence rather than unique target specificity. Include venetoclax–azacitidine as the established metabolic comparator [S3]. Track initially immature leukemic cells longitudinally using morphology, maturation-marker panels, and leukemia-associated genotypes, thereby distinguishing induced differentiation from selective survival of pre-existing mature cells. Assess apoptosis separately. After washout, measure clonogenic recovery and leukemia initiation by transplantation, rather than calling marker changes stem-cell eradication. Exposure-matched residual-disease xenografts should assess marrow pharmacology and propagation, alongside normal-progenitor colony formation and engraftment.

Alternative explanations include off-target cytotoxicity, reversible growth arrest, or selective elimination of immature cells masquerading as differentiation. Oxidative metabolism might mark chemotherapy stress without predicting pyrimidine dependence. Physiological uridine availability could also erase sensitivity observed in artificial culture. Temporal measurements, lineage tracking, genetic perturbation, and rescue distinguish these possibilities.

Falsification and limitations: A decisive falsifying observation would be confirmed DHODH inhibition and pyrimidine depletion at attainable exposure in preclassified salvage-poor residual AML, without differentiation or reduced leukemia-propagating capacity. Comparable impairment of normal progenitor function would reject the proposed selective window. Effects restricted to unattainable concentrations would defeat the translational hypothesis even if the cellular mechanism were supported. Sample heterogeneity, changing salvage capacity, transplantation biases, and uncertain marrow exposure limit generalization; positive preclinical results would not establish clinical efficacy.
