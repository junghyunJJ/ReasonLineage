# c004

Teriflunomide-induced differentiation in uridine-salvage-limited residual AML

Hypothesis. In cytarabine-persistent AML with retained oxidative metabolism and limited uridine-salvage capacity, teriflunomide will inhibit DHODH, deplete pyrimidines, and induce leukemic myeloid differentiation, reducing leukemia-propagating capacity at exposures that preserve normal hematopoietic progenitor function. This is a context-specific repurposing hypothesis, not a claim that DHODH inhibition is newly discovered or that mitochondrial inhibition generally provides AML selectivity.

Supported premises. Cytarabine-resistant AML cells displayed oxidative-metabolism-associated features in the studied models, but were not consistently enriched for immature, quiescent leukemia stem cells [S2]. Separately, DHODH inhibition promoted differentiation and affected disease-related endpoints in experimental AML [S4]. These observations justify testing differentiation in metabolically characterized residual disease; they do not establish that those residual cells depend on DHODH. ROS-low, BCL-2-associated leukemia stem cells constitute a separately demonstrated metabolic context [S1]. Accordingly, neither residual-cell abundance nor a ROS-low phenotype will substitute for functional measurements of leukemia propagation. Venetoclax–azacitidine provides an established metabolic comparator, not the proposed repurposing intervention [S3].

Drug identity and original indication. Unverified prior knowledge requiring confirmation: teriflunomide is an approved treatment for relapsing forms of multiple sclerosis and inhibits DHODH. Also unverified prior knowledge: DHODH participates in de novo pyrimidine synthesis, whereas uridine salvage can bypass that requirement. The packet does not verify these pharmacological details, regulatory status, target potency, or clinically achievable free exposure. They must be checked before advancing the proposal; no numerical exposure claim is assumed here.

Speculative bridges. The central assumptions are that a subset of oxidative residual AML remains dependent on de novo pyrimidine production, that insufficient salvage exposes this dependence under marrow-relevant conditions, and that pyrimidine restriction permits differentiation rather than merely causing death. Preferential preservation of normal progenitors is an additional, unproven requirement. Oxidative metabolism alone is not proposed as a sufficient response biomarker.

Discriminating preclinical strategy. Obtain paired pretreatment and post-cytarabine AML specimens where feasible, alongside independent residual-disease models and healthy-donor hematopoietic progenitors. Before drug treatment, use isotope tracing to classify pyrimidine synthesis and uridine-salvage capacity independently of subsequent response; measure respiratory activity without assigning stem-cell identity from metabolism alone. Study both low- and high-salvage AML under measured, marrow-relevant uridine conditions, including stromal coculture.

After verifying pharmacology, define an exposure ceiling using clinically supported unbound concentrations and measured culture binding. Supra-ceiling experiments may clarify mechanism but cannot establish translational feasibility. Compare teriflunomide with vehicle, an orthogonal DHODH inhibitor, a mitochondrial-stress control, and venetoclax–azacitidine as the established comparator [S3]. Measure early DHODH-pathway metabolite changes and pyrimidine flux before apoptosis, followed by morphology, myeloid-marker acquisition, cell-cycle changes, and viability. Confirm that differentiated cells carry the leukemia-associated genotype, excluding apparent maturation caused by selective blast loss or normal-cell enrichment.

Require mechanistic triangulation: uridine supplementation should restore pyrimidine pools and reverse differentiation; DHODH suppression should reproduce the relevant response; and a biochemically validated inhibitor-resistant DHODH rescue construct in tractable models should prevent it. Assess clonogenic output and limiting-dilution leukemia initiation after drug washout, using equal viable-cell inputs. Test normal progenitor colony formation and long-term engraftment at matched exposure. Differentiation, bulk killing, and loss of leukemia-propagating function must be reported separately.

Alternatives and falsification. Apparent benefit could instead reflect nonspecific mitochondrial injury, off-target toxicity, or selection of pre-existing mature cells. Confirmed DHODH inhibition and pyrimidine depletion within the exposure ceiling, without increased leukemic differentiation or reduced leukemia initiation in prespecified low-salvage samples, would falsify the central hypothesis. Persistence of benefit after validated target bypass would reject the proposed mechanism; comparable normal-progenitor injury would reject the proposed selectivity.

Limitations. Extracellular uridine or stromal support might erase the dependency. Free marrow exposure may be inadequate, and differentiation may leave disease-propagating clones intact. Specimen heterogeneity, culture-induced metabolic changes, and transplantation constraints limit generalization. A positive result would justify further pharmacological development, not establish clinical AML efficacy.
