DHODH-Dependent Differentiation of Pyrimidine-Salvage-Limited Residual AML with Teriflunomide

Hypothesis. In post-cytarabine residual AML with high oxidative metabolism and limited pyrimidine-salvage capacity, teriflunomide will induce durable myeloid differentiation through DHODH-dependent pyrimidine depletion, reducing leukemia-propagating capacity at exposures that spare normal hematopoietic progenitors. This proposes a phenotype-restricted repurposing opportunity, not universal AML activity or a claim of previously undiscovered drug–AML effects.

Supported premises. Cytarabine-resistant AML cells displayed oxidative-metabolism-associated features in the investigated models, but were not consistently enriched for immature, quiescent leukemia stem cells [S2]. Separately, DHODH inhibition promoted differentiation and affected disease-related endpoints in experimental AML [S4]. These findings justify testing differentiation in metabolically characterized residual disease; they do not establish that oxidative metabolism predicts DHODH dependence. BCL-2-associated oxidative-phosphorylation dependence in functionally defined, frequently ROS-low leukemia stem cells provides additional motivation for metabolic targeting, without establishing a therapeutic window for another agent [S1]. Venetoclax–azacitidine is an established metabolic comparator, not the proposed repurposing discovery [S3].

Unverified prior knowledge. Teriflunomide is recalled to be approved for relapsing forms of multiple sclerosis and to inhibit DHODH, an enzyme supporting de novo pyrimidine synthesis. Extracellular uridine is recalled to permit bypass through pyrimidine salvage. These drug, indication, and biochemical facts are outside the packet and require independent confirmation before implementation. No approved-dose marrow exposure or AML-selective concentration is established here.

Speculative bridges. The central assumption is that some oxidative residual AML populations remain dependent on de novo pyrimidine production because salvage cannot meet their requirements. A second assumption is that partial depletion releases a differentiation blockade before causing generalized injury. A third is that normal progenitors possess a sufficient functional margin at clinically attainable exposure. None follows automatically from [S2] or [S4]. Low salvage must therefore be measured rather than inferred from oxygen consumption, stem-cell markers, or treatment resistance.

Discriminating preclinical strategy. Obtain paired pretreatment and post-cytarabine patient-derived AML populations across independent donors, retaining matched untreated controls. Measure oxygen consumption, de novo-versus-salvage pyrimidine flux, and nucleotide pools before drug exposure. Prespecify oxidative, low-salvage residual cells as the primary context; include oxidative, high-salvage cells and nonoxidative residual cells as biological comparators. Establish concentration ceilings using verified human unbound pharmacokinetics and measured marrow exposure, rather than nominal culture concentrations. Test protein-adjusted exposures in stromal coculture alongside normal marrow CD34-positive cells, including resting and proliferation-matched conditions.

Measure biochemical DHODH inhibition and pyrimidine depletion before assessing maturation, early apoptosis, and proliferation. Require concordant morphology and differentiation-marker changes in genotype-confirmed leukemic cells, not merely expansion of contaminating normal cells. After washout, assess persistence of maturation, clonogenic output, and limiting-dilution leukemia propagation using equal viable leukemic-cell inputs. Reduced bulk viability alone does not demonstrate differentiation or stem-cell depletion. Include vehicle, an independently validated DHODH inhibitor, genetic DHODH suppression, and venetoclax–azacitidine as comparators. Test uridine rescue after demonstrating salvage uptake; where technically achievable, use a biochemically validated, catalytically functional teriflunomide-resistant DHODH rescue to strengthen target attribution.

Alternatives and falsification. Apparent maturation could reflect preferential killing of immature cells, nonspecific mitochondrial injury, proliferation differences, or stromal effects. Time-resolved tracking of initially immature leukemic cells and target-rescue experiments distinguish these explanations. The mechanistic hypothesis would be falsified if verified DHODH inhibition and pyrimidine depletion at attainable exposure fail to induce durable leukemic differentiation in the prespecified context. Persistence of the differentiation effect despite restoration of pyrimidine pools would contradict the proposed mediator. Equivalent impairment of normal progenitor function would falsify the proposed selective window even if AML differentiation occurred.

Limitations. Donor heterogeneity, culture-dependent salvage, marrow nutrient availability, and transplantation artifacts may restrict interpretation. Approval records, pharmacology, protein binding, marrow distribution, and systemic toxicity require verification. Positive preclinical findings would support further investigation, not establish clinical efficacy or justify off-label treatment.
