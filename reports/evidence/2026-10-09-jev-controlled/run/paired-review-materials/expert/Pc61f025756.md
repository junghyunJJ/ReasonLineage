# Pc61f025756

Teriflunomide-Induced Differentiation in Pyrimidine-Salvage-Limited, Cytarabine-Persistent AML

Hypothesis: At clinically attainable unbound exposure, teriflunomide will induce durable myeloid differentiation and reduce clonogenicity in cytarabine-persistent AML with limited uridine-salvage capacity, through DHODH-dependent pyrimidine depletion. Salvage-competent AML and normal hematopoietic progenitors should be less affected. This proposes a context-specific differentiation treatment, not an established AML regimen or universal stem-cell therapy.

Unverified prior knowledge requiring confirmation: teriflunomide is approved for relapsing forms of multiple sclerosis, its original indication, and inhibits DHODH, an enzyme involved in de novo pyrimidine synthesis. These drug-specific statements are not established by the supplied packet. Approval details, biochemical selectivity, human unbound exposure, and clinically relevant exposure duration must be checked before translational advancement; no concentration is assumed to be achievable merely because the drug is approved.

Supported premises: DHODH inhibition promoted myeloid differentiation and affected disease-related endpoints in experimental AML systems [S4]. Separately, cytarabine-resistant cells displayed oxidative-metabolism-associated features but were not consistently enriched for immature, quiescent leukemia stem cells [S2]. The ROS-low, BCL-2-associated stem-cell population studied in S1 therefore cannot simply be equated with this residual population [S1, S2]. Venetoclax–azacitidine supplies an established metabolism-focused comparator, not evidence that teriflunomide works [S3]. None of these sources establishes teriflunomide efficacy, its proposed salvage-defined selectivity, or acceptable exposure.

Speculative bridge: a subset of cytarabine-persistent AML may retain substantial de novo pyrimidine dependence while having insufficient extracellular-uridine salvage. In that context, teriflunomide-mediated DHODH inhibition could lower pyrimidine availability sufficiently to release the differentiation blockade before causing generalized cell death. Oxidative metabolism is an enrichment feature to examine, not proof of DHODH dependence. The proposed normal-cell advantage is also an assumption: normal progenitors might instead share the dependency.

Discriminating preclinical strategy: study paired pretreatment and cytarabine-persistent primary AML specimens, alongside normal donor hematopoietic stem/progenitor cells. Maintain physiological uridine and protein conditions rather than artificially favoring de novo synthesis. Define salvage-low and salvage-high groups prospectively using labeled-uridine incorporation and complementary de novo flux measurements, with thresholds fixed before examining drug response. Measure oxidative metabolism separately rather than using it as a surrogate for salvage. Test teriflunomide concentration–time profiles constrained by subsequently verified human free-drug exposure.

Establish temporal ordering through direct DHODH activity measurements, pyrimidine-pool measurements, respiration, viability, morphology, and maturation-marker expression. Genotype-confirmed leukemic progeny should acquire mature morphology and lose serial clonogenicity after washout; marker shifts alone are insufficient. Reduced leukemia-initiating activity would require transplantation evidence and must not be inferred from bulk-cell differentiation. Include vehicle, an orthogonal DHODH perturbation, venetoclax–azacitidine, and identically exposed normal progenitors. Uridine supplementation should restore pyrimidine pools and prevent differentiation if depletion mediates the effect. For stronger drug-specific attribution, develop and validate a catalytically competent, teriflunomide-resistant DHODH construct and test whether it rescues both metabolic and differentiation endpoints. Advance responsive specimens into patient-derived models with measured unbound exposure and marrow toxicity.

Alternatives include off-target cytotoxicity, generalized mitochondrial stress, apparent maturation caused by selective survival of pre-existing mature cells, or expansion of contaminating normal cells. Early target engagement, rescue experiments, single-cell lineage tracking, and leukemic-genotype confirmation distinguish these explanations from genuine differentiation.

Falsification and limitations: confirmed DHODH inhibition and pyrimidine depletion at relevant exposure without durable maturation or reduced clonogenicity in prospectively defined salvage-low residual AML would falsify the central prediction. Persistence of differentiation despite successful drug-resistant-DHODH rescue would reject the proposed mechanism. Comparable injury to normal progenitors would falsify the predicted therapeutic selectivity. Marrow metabolite availability, heterogeneous salvage, achievable exposure, and culture-induced state changes may defeat translation. Even successful preclinical results would establish neither clinical benefit nor eradication of every AML stem-cell population.
