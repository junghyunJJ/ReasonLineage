# P2b67f0f74d

Atovaquone-induced pyrimidine starvation differentiates oxidative, uridine-salvage-poor AML persisters

Hypothesis. Sequential atovaquone exposure after cytarabine will induce DHODH-mediated pyrimidine depletion and genuine myeloid differentiation, reducing durable clonogenicity in residual AML cells with high oxidative metabolism and weak extracellular-uridine salvage. The predicted effect must occur at achievable unbound marrow exposure, not merely at a high nominal culture concentration. This is not a claim that all residual cells are leukemia stem cells.

Supported premises. Cytarabine-resistant cells in the studied models displayed oxidative-metabolism-associated properties without consistent enrichment for immature, quiescent stem cells [S2]. DHODH inhibition promoted differentiation in experimental AML systems [S4]. Separately, BCL-2 inhibition affected oxidative phosphorylation in ROS-low, functionally defined leukemia stem cells [S1]. That observation motivates examining selectivity but does not establish atovaquone’s activity or therapeutic window. Venetoclax–azacitidine supplies an established metabolic comparator, not the proposed repurposing discovery [S3].

Unverified prior knowledge and speculative bridge. Atovaquone is recalled as approved for Pneumocystis pneumonia treatment and prevention, its non-AML indication; this approval requires verification. Its reported inhibition of mitochondrial complex III and mammalian DHODH’s dependence on oxidized ubiquinone are also unverified prior knowledge. The proposed causal chain is that atovaquone limits ubiquinone reoxidation, thereby restricting DHODH-dependent pyrimidine synthesis sufficiently to release a differentiation blockade before generalized energy failure. This chain is an assumption, not an S1–S4 finding. High respiration alone need not predict susceptibility; inadequate uridine salvage is proposed as the additional vulnerability.

Discriminating preclinical tests. Use paired untreated and cytarabine-residual primary AML cells generated in patient-derived models, with independent donors as biological replicates. Measure respiration and isotope-labeled extracellular-uridine contribution to pyrimidine pools before treatment. Prospectively stratify residual cells by oxidative metabolism and salvage capacity rather than surface phenotype alone. Profile ROS-low fractions separately without inferring stem-cell activity from phenotype.

Compare sequential atovaquone with vehicle, an independently validated DHODH inhibitor, and venetoclax–azacitidine. Anchor concentrations to independently verified human unbound exposure, then establish whether corresponding marrow exposure is achievable in the animal model. Include normal donor CD34-positive progenitors under matched conditions and physiologically relevant extracellular uridine concentrations.

Collect early complex-III activity, ubiquinone redox state, respiration, dihydroorotate/orotate balance, and pyrimidine-pool measurements before substantial apoptosis. Subsequently measure morphology, multiple maturation markers, and single-cell trajectories to distinguish differentiation from selective survival of pre-existing mature cells. Assess colony formation after washout using equal viable-cell inputs. Measure residual leukemia-initiating activity by limiting-dilution transplantation separately: neither maturation nor bulk-cell depletion alone demonstrates stem-cell elimination.

For causal discrimination, restore pyrimidine pools with uridine while verifying uptake. Where salvage cannot support rescue, test a biochemically validated respiratory-chain-independent DHODH bypass, with an inactive-construct control. The critical prediction is that pyrimidine restoration prevents atovaquone-induced maturation and clonogenic loss while complex-III inhibition and respiratory suppression persist. Genetic DHODH suppression should phenocopy the maturation component, with pathway engagement documented.

Alternatives and falsification. Apparent efficacy could instead reflect generalized energy depletion, unrelated drug effects, or selective killing of blasts. Consequently, rescue must correct pyrimidine pools without correcting respiration. At an exposure producing maturation before appreciable cell death, if verified pyrimidine restoration leaves maturation and clonogenic loss unchanged, the proposed DHODH-mediated mechanism is falsified. A respiratory decline without preceding pyrimidine depletion would likewise contradict the specified causal sequence.

Limitations. Protein binding, formulation, marrow distribution, and relevant achievable exposure are unresolved here, not assumed favorable. Activity requiring supraclinical unbound concentrations would defeat the exposure-constrained proposal. Compare normal colony formation and long-term hematopoietic engraftment before claiming selectivity; normal cells may share the vulnerability. Donor heterogeneity and extracellular uridine could erase the predicted response. These experiments could establish or reject a context-specific mechanism, but would not establish clinical benefit.
