# P5fb75a0e0e

Low uridine salvage sensitizes cytarabine-residual AML to teriflunomide-induced differentiation

Hypothesis: In cytarabine-residual AML with an oxidative metabolic phenotype, persistent differentiation blockade, and low functional uridine-salvage capacity, clinically achievable unbound teriflunomide exposure will inhibit DHODH, deplete pyrimidine pools, and induce durable myeloid differentiation. This should reduce leukemia-propagating capacity while relatively sparing normal hematopoietic progenitors. Low salvage capacity, rather than oxidative metabolism alone, is the proposed determinant of sensitivity.

Supported premises: DHODH inhibition promoted differentiation and affected disease-related endpoints in experimental AML systems [S4]. Cytarabine-resistant AML cells displayed oxidative-metabolism-associated properties but were not consistently enriched for immature, quiescent leukemia stem cells [S2]. These findings justify investigating a differentiation-directed metabolic intervention in residual disease, but neither establishes teriflunomide efficacy. The ROS-low, BCL-2-associated stem-cell population described in [S1] must therefore be analyzed separately rather than equated with all residual AML. Venetoclax–azacitidine provides an established energy-metabolism comparator, not a new repurposing proposal [S3].

Drug premise and speculative bridges: Teriflunomide’s approval for relapsing forms of multiple sclerosis and its DHODH-inhibitory activity are unverified prior knowledge here; the packet does not verify either claim. Another unverified biochemical premise is that extracellular uridine salvage can replenish pyrimidines despite DHODH inhibition. Regulatory status, target pharmacology, and human unbound exposure require independent confirmation before experimentation. The central speculative bridge is that a subset of residual AML cannot salvage enough uridine under marrow conditions to compensate for DHODH inhibition. Further assumptions are that achievable drug exposure crosses this metabolic threshold, nucleotide depletion produces differentiation rather than only arrest or death, and normal progenitors retain sufficient compensatory capacity. Oxidative metabolism is not assumed to prove DHODH dependence.

Discriminating preclinical strategy: Study paired pretreatment and cytarabine-residual patient-derived AML samples, supplemented by residual-disease xenografts. Before drug treatment, measure respiration and quantify functional salvage using labeled uridine incorporation into pyrimidine nucleotides under physiologically justified uridine conditions. Include both low- and high-salvage AML, and normal donor CD34-positive progenitors. Use stromal coculture and protein-containing media, directly measure unbound drug, and restrict efficacy conclusions to independently verified human-compatible exposures. Establish temporal ordering by measuring DHODH-pathway metabolites and nucleotide pools before apoptosis, respiratory collapse, or maturation. Score differentiation using blinded morphology and mature-myeloid immunophenotypes in cells confirmed to retain the leukemia genotype; this guards against mistaking surviving normal cells or selective blast killing for malignant-cell differentiation.

Mechanistic discrimination: Compare teriflunomide with vehicle, an independently characterized DHODH inhibitor, and established venetoclax–azacitidine [S3]. DHODH suppression should phenocopy the differentiation response. Uridine supplementation should reverse differentiation when isotope tracing confirms restoration of pyrimidine pools, without reducing drug exposure. These interventions distinguish on-pathway nucleotide limitation from unrelated drug toxicity. Measure viable malignant-cell output, apoptosis, and colony formation separately. In randomized xenografts, determine free plasma and marrow exposure, then test leukemia-propagating capacity by limiting-dilution secondary transplantation after drug washout. Assess normal progenitor colony formation and normal hematopoietic engraftment in parallel; reduced AML viability alone does not demonstrate selectivity or stem-cell depletion.

Alternatives, falsification, and limitations: Apparent maturation could reflect differential survival of pre-existing mature cells, generic cytostasis, or off-target toxicity. Marrow-derived uridine could abolish a response seen in simplified culture. The central falsifying observation would be confirmed DHODH engagement and sustained pyrimidine depletion in predicted low-salvage residual AML without genuine differentiation or reduced leukemia-propagating capacity. Persistence of the response after verified pyrimidine-pool rescue would instead contradict the proposed causal mechanism. Failure to engage DHODH at human-compatible exposure would reject translational feasibility. Heterogeneous salvage capacity, microenvironmental rescue, uncertain marrow pharmacokinetics, and normal-cell toxicity remain major limitations. The packet supports a pathway rationale, not this drug’s therapeutic window, clinical benefit, or novelty.
