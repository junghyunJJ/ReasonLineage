Atovaquone-driven DHODH restriction induces differentiation in oxidative cytarabine-residual AML

Hypothesis: At clinically attainable unbound exposure, atovaquone will inhibit respiratory-complex-III-supported DHODH activity sufficiently to induce differentiation and reduce leukemia propagation in cytarabine-residual AML with persistent oxidative metabolism and limited pyrimidine salvage. The predicted effect is mediated by pyrimidine depletion, rather than generalized ATP collapse. This concerns a defined residual-cell context, not all AML or an assumed leukemia-stem-cell population.

Supported premises: Cytarabine-resistant AML cells displayed oxidative-metabolism-associated features in the studied models, but were not consistently enriched for immature, quiescent leukemia stem cells [S2]. Separately, DHODH inhibition promoted myeloid differentiation in experimental AML systems [S4]. Together, these findings motivate testing a respiratory-to-pyrimidine connection; they do not establish that oxidative residual cells are especially DHODH-dependent. The ROS-low, BCL-2-associated population described in [S1] should be assessed separately rather than used as a synonym for residual disease. Venetoclax–azacitidine provides an established metabolic comparator, not the proposed repurposing discovery [S3].

Drug rationale and speculative bridges: Unverified prior knowledge requiring independent checking is that atovaquone was originally approved for treatment of Pneumocystis pneumonia, can inhibit mammalian mitochondrial complex III under some experimental conditions, and has substantial protein binding. Also unverified within this packet are the biochemical premises that mitochondrial DHODH requires oxidized ubiquinone and that uridine salvage can bypass de novo pyrimidine synthesis. The central speculative bridge is that atovaquone-induced restriction of ubiquinone oxidation will suppress DHODH before causing nonspecific energetic failure. A second, explicitly uncertain bridge is that salvage-limited residual AML will differentiate under this restriction while normal hematopoietic progenitors retain sufficient functional capacity. Neither bridge follows automatically from an oxidative phenotype.

Discriminating preclinical strategy: Use multiple primary AML specimens exposed to a cytarabine pulse followed by washout, alongside matched untreated AML and normal marrow progenitors in comparable cycling states. Before testing response, classify residual samples by oxygen consumption and labeled-uridine incorporation, with a prespecified oxidative, salvage-low subgroup. Measure free atovaquone in protein-containing culture conditions and restrict translational conclusions to independently verified achievable unbound exposures. Early measurements should include complex III activity, ubiquinone redox state, dihydroorotate-to-orotate balance, pyrimidine-synthesis flux, nucleotide pools, ATP, and apoptosis. Separate biochemical assays of complex III and DHODH should test whether the drug acts through respiration rather than directly inhibiting DHODH.

Later endpoints should distinguish maturation from selective killing: follow leukemia-genotype-confirmed cells through morphology and a prespecified myeloid differentiation-marker panel, track absolute viable cell numbers, and assess clonogenicity after drug washout. Compare vehicle, a reference DHODH inhibitor, and venetoclax–azacitidine as the established comparator [S3]. The decisive epistasis experiments are uridine supplementation and expression of a validated respiratory-chain-independent DHODH construct. Both should restore pyrimidine pools and prevent atovaquone-induced maturation and propagation loss without restoring respiratory inhibition. Rescue performance must first be validated against the reference DHODH inhibitor. If these conditions hold, extend testing to patient-derived xenografts, measuring marrow exposure, differentiation, secondary-transplant leukemia initiation, and normal hematopoietic recovery. Reduced bulk viability alone would not establish differentiation or stem-cell elimination.

Alternatives and falsification: Apparent maturation could reflect preferential death of immature cells, delayed cytarabine injury, direct DHODH inhibition, or unrelated atovaquone targets. Genotype-resolved cell tracking, direct enzyme assays, temporal ordering, and pyrimidine rescue distinguish these possibilities. A decisive falsifying observation would be persistent differentiation and propagation loss despite both validated rescues restoring pyrimidine pools, while the same rescues reverse the reference inhibitor’s effects. That result would reject the proposed DHODH-dependent mechanism even if atovaquone remained cytotoxic.

Limitations: Physiological uridine availability, marrow exposure, heterogeneous metabolic states, and injury to proliferating normal progenitors may eliminate any therapeutic window. Effects occurring only above achievable unbound exposure would reject translational feasibility; comparable normal-progenitor damage would reject predicted selectivity. Differentiation need not produce durable leukemia control. Approval details, human exposure, rescue-construct properties, and existing AML studies of atovaquone require verification outside this frozen packet; novelty is therefore not established.
