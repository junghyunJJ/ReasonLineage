## Hypothesis: Repurposing teriflunomide (an approved multiple sclerosis therapy) as a DHODH inhibitor to relieve the differentiation block in AML blasts

### Biological context and central hypothesis

AML blasts fail to undergo terminal myeloid differentiation. DHODH is a mitochondrial enzyme that performs the fourth step of de novo pyrimidine biosynthesis by donating electrons to CoQ in the respiratory chain. Inhibiting DHODH depletes intracellular orotate-to-UMP flux and has been shown (S4) to induce myeloid differentiation in preclinical AML models. Teriflunomide (marketed as Aubagio for relapsing–remitting multiple sclerosis; the active metabolite of leflunomide, Arava, approved for rheumatoid arthritis) is an oral DHODH inhibitor with years of chronic use in non-oncology patients. The hypothesis is that teriflunomide, at exposures near those achieved with approved MS dosing, will inhibit DHODH in primary AML blasts, deplete pyrimidine pools, and induce myeloid differentiation markers (CD11b/CD14/CD15) rather than nonspecific cytotoxicity, offering a non-myelosuppressive, orally available differentiation therapy without new chemical development.

**Original indication:** Relapsing forms of multiple sclerosis (teriflunomide/Aubagio). This and related regulatory details are unverified prior knowledge requiring confirmation via current FDA/EMA labels.

### Supported premises from the evidence packet

- **S4** directly demonstrates that DHODH inhibition promotes myeloid differentiation in AML experimental systems and affects disease-related endpoints independent of nonspecific cell killing. This is the mechanistic anchor.
- **S1** shows quiescent LSCs are ROS-low, BCL-2-high, and OxPhos-dependent, establishing that metabolic vulnerabilities distinguish malignant from normal hematopoietic populations.
- **S2** demonstrates that cytarabine-resistant residual AML cells carry oxidative-metabolism features (mitochondrial, FAO), identifying a biological state of therapeutic relevance beyond stemness alone.
- **S3** (venetoclax–azacitidine) provides an established clinical comparator showing that targeting LSC energy metabolism produces clinically meaningful effects; this supports the metabolic-targeting framework generally but does not validate teriflunomide specifically.

### Speculative bridges requiring validation

1. **Pharmacologic bridge:** Teriflunomide's reported steady-state plasma levels (on the order of tens of micromolar; unverified) may exceed the IC₅₀ for DHODH, but whether sufficient intracellular exposure is achieved in bone-marrow-resident AML blasts in vivo is unknown.
2. **Selectivity via differential salvage:** AML blasts may have insufficient nucleoside-salvage capacity to compensate for DHODH inhibition, whereas normal CD34⁺ progenitors upregulate salvage. This derives from S4's models but has not been validated across AML genotypes with teriflunomide.
3. **OxPhos–DHODH flux coupling:** Because DHODH donates electrons to CoQ, cells with high oxidative metabolism (per S2) may exhibit greater DHODH flux and greater vulnerability to inhibition. This coupling is theoretical and not directly tested in the packet.
4. **LSC extension:** Whether DHODH inhibition affects the quiescent ROS-low population described in S1, or only proliferating blasts, is unknown—quiescent LSCs may have lower pyrimidine demand.

### Alternative explanations

- **Off-target kinase inhibition:** A77 1726/teriflunomide has been reported (unverified prior knowledge) to inhibit kinases such as JAK3 or TYK2; any differentiation effect could arise from these off-targets rather than DHODH. Uridine-rescue experiments discriminate this.
- **General anti-metabolite effect:** Any nucleotide-depleting agent might produce similar marker changes without DHODH-specific dependence.
- **Microenvironmental effects:** Teriflunomide could alter the bone-marrow niche rather than acting directly on AML cells.

### Discriminating preclinical validation strategy

1. Treat primary AML blasts (≥10 genetically diverse samples) and normal donor CD34⁺ HSPCs with teriflunomide across a dose range; measure DHODH engagement by LC-MS (dihydroorotate accumulation, UMP depletion) at 6–24 hours.
2. Test mechanism specificity: (a) excess exogenous uridine should rescue differentiation if DHODH-dependent; (b) brequinar or a second DHODH inhibitor should phenocopy; (c) DHODH knockdown or overexpression should sensitize or protect.
3. Assess differentiation (CD11b/CD14/CD15, morphology, NBT reduction) at days 5–7; compare to vehicle and to venetoclax–azacitidine (S3) as a non-differentiation metabolic comparator.
4. Test whether teriflunomide affects the cytarabine-resistant residual OxPhos-high population identified per S2.
5. Parallel toxicity in normal CD34⁺ cells: CFU-GM, BFU-E, and long-term culture-initiating cell assays.

**Discriminating test:** If uridine rescues the differentiation phenotype AND a structurally distinct DHODH inhibitor phenocopies, DHODH is the operative target. If uridine does not rescue, the mechanism is likely off-target.

### Falsifying outcome

The hypothesis is disconfirmed if:
- Teriflunomide at plasma-achievable concentrations fails to accumulate dihydroorotate or deplete UMP in primary AML blasts (no target engagement), or
- It causes loss of viability without concurrent CD11b/CD14 induction (nonspecific cytotoxicity, not differentiation), or
- Uridine does not rescue the differentiation phenotype (mechanism is not DHODH-dependent), or
- Normal CD34⁺ progenitors are eliminated at concentrations that spare or minimally affect AML blasts (no therapeutic window).

### Selectivity and translational limitations

- Teriflunomide's long half-life (reported ~2 weeks due to enterohepatic recirculation; unverified) complicates rapid reversibility if toxicity occurs.
- Known risks include hepatotoxicity and teratogenicity (unverified from this packet), constraining use in younger patients and those with liver dysfunction.
- All DHODH-supporting evidence comes from prechemical models (S4), not from teriflunomide itself; clinical DHODH inhibitors may already exist in development for AML (unverified prior knowledge), reducing novelty.
- AML genotypes with enhanced nucleoside transport or high salvage flux may be intrinsically resistant.
- No clinical trial has tested this drug in AML; the entire therapeutic hypothesis is inferential.

### Verified facts vs. unverified prior knowledge

**Verified from packet:** DHODH inhibition causes differentiation in AML models (S4); LSCs are ROS-low, BCL-2-high, OxPhos-dependent (S1); chemo-resistant cells are OxPhos-high (S2); ven–aza disrupts LSC metabolism (S3).

**Unverified prior knowledge:** Teriflunomide's approved indication wording, FDA approval date, plasma pharmacokinetics, half-life, and boxed warnings; clinical-stage DHODH inhibitor pipeline for AML; off-target kinase profile of A77 1726.

1) S4 (PMID 27641501) is the mechanistic anchor: DHODH inhibition promoted myeloid differentiation in experimental AML systems and affected disease-related endpoints. 2) S2 (PMID 28416471) defines a plausible context: cytarabine-resistant residual cells were not reliably enriched for immature LSCs but showed oxidative-metabolism and fatty-acid-oxidation features; such committed, still-cycling cells plausibly retain strong dependence on de novo pyrimidine supply, making them testable targets. 3) S1 (PMID 23333149) defines the contrasting population: functionally defined LSCs were frequently ROS-low, quiescent, and BCL-2-high, implying a different dependency and a predicted differential effect of a replication-dependent mechanism. 4) S3 (PMID 30420752) supplies the established comparator: venetoclax with azacitidine alters LSC energy metabolism (TCA cycle, oxidative phosphorylation); that regimen is not the proposal here, and any teriflunomide effect must be shown to be non-redundant. Explicitly speculative bridges
(i) Clinically achievable marrow exposure of teriflunomide inhibits DHODH sufficiently in patient blasts; packet data provide no exposure facts, and standard teriflunomide dosing, long half-life, and slow clearance are recalled and unverified. (ii) The differentiation program seen with experimental DHODH inhibitors in S4 generalizes pharmacologically to this approved molecule in vivo. (iii) Residual post-cytarabine disease remains salvage-insufficient and DHODH-dependent in the marrow niche. (iv) Sequencing teriflunomide after cytarabine could convert residual cycling cells toward differentiation; this translational sequencing claim is speculative.

Alternative explanations
Off-target actions of teriflunomide, including recalled reports of activity beyond DHODH on kinase pathways (unverified prior knowledge), could drive observed biology. Nonspecific pyrimidine starvation might kill cycling cells, with apparent differentiation merely reflecting selective survival of more mature cells rather than lineage reprogramming. The dominant residual-cell dependency could instead be oxidative phosphorylation/fatty-acid oxidation [S2], better targeted by a different drug class; DHODH activity, though coupled to the respiratory chain, is upstream of respiration. Normal hematopoietic progenitors may be equally sensitive, giving myelosuppression without a therapeutic window. Salvage-pathway heterogeneity across patients may blunt efficacy in subsets.

Discriminating preclinical validation strategy
Test a panel of primary AML specimens (de novo and relapsed/refractory, diverse genetics) plus normal CD34+ progenitor controls. Arms: teriflunomide; a reference DHODH inhibitor of the S4 type (e.g., brequinar-class); a venetoclax-containing arm as metabolic comparator per S3. Concentrations should be anchored to verified human plasma exposure before interpretation. Readouts: (a) on-target pharmacology — LC-MS measurement of intracellular pyrimidine/UMP pools and uridine add-back rescue; if exogenous uridine rescues both growth suppression and differentiation-marker induction, the mechanism is DHODH-dependent, whereas failure to rescue indicates off-target activity; (b) differentiation endpoints — CD11b/CD14/CD15 induction, morphology, colony-forming and serial-replating assays; (c) stem-cell endpoints — frequency of the ROS-low, HSC-marker-enriched LSC population before and after treatment, framed by S1, alongside TCA/OXPHOS signatures to test nonredundancy with S3-like effects; (d) residual-disease arm — cytarabine pretreatment followed by teriflunomide on residual cells, with survival and differentiation endpoints per S2; (e) normal-cell window — CFU-GM and long-term culture of normal CD34+ cells, with a required differential between AML and normal progenitors.

Falsifying observation (any one disconfirms)
Teriflunomide fails to deplete pyrimidine pools or induce differentiation markers at clinically achievable concentrations; uridine add-back fails to rescue, indicating the effect is not DHODH-mediated; activity appears only far above verified human exposure; normal progenitors are as sensitive as AML blasts; or cytarabine-pretreated residual cells show neither differentiation nor burden reduction, rejecting the proposed drug–mechanism–context relationship.

Limitations
Exposure, half-life, and safety facts (including teratogenicity and slow drug elimination) are recollections requiring verification. Ex vivo systems may not reproduce the marrow niche; S4 does not guarantee that every approved DHODH-affecting drug reproduces the effect or achieves suitable exposure [S4]. This hypothesis addresses differentiation and bulk/residual disease; it does not claim eradication of quiescent ROS-low LSCs [S1], so durable effects might still require combination with established regimens — a separate question. Clinical development of other DHODH inhibitors in AML is plausible prior knowledge and must be verified to keep this proposal distinct from novel-agent trials rather than a repackaged standard approach.
